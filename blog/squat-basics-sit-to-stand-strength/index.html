Squat Basics: Building the Strength to Stand Up Easily for Life

Every time you get up from a chair, a car or the toilet, you're doing a squat. Training it well keeps everyday life easy.

The squat might sound like a gym exercise, but it's one of the most important movements in daily life. Getting out of a chair, climbing into a car, lowering yourself onto a couch and picking up a child all rely on squat strength. When that strength fades, people start pushing off armrests, rocking to stand up or avoiding low seats altogether.

The good news: the squat is very trainable at any age, and you can start right at home.

Start with the sit-to-stand

The simplest squat is standing up from a chair without using your hands. Sit near the front edge of a sturdy chair, feet about hip-width apart and flat on the floor. Lean your chest slightly forward, press through your whole foot and stand up tall. Then slowly lower back down with control. Aim for 8 to 10 reps.

If that's hard, use your hands lightly on your thighs or a higher seat. If it's easy, lower down more slowly or hold a weight at your chest.

Form tips that make a big difference

  • Keep your weight spread across your whole foot, not just your toes
  • Let your knees track in line with your toes rather than caving inward
  • Push your hips back as you lower, like you're reaching for the chair
  • Keep your chest up and your back long
  • Breathe out as you stand up

Ways to progress

Once chair squats feel comfortable, you can gradually make them more challenging:

  • Use a lower chair or box
  • Slow down the lowering part to 3 or 4 seconds
  • Pause for a second just above the chair
  • Hold a dumbbell or kettlebell at your chest, known as a goblet squat
  • Progress to split squats or step-ups for single-leg strength

Change one thing at a time and keep your form solid as the challenge increases.

What if squats bother your knees?

Squatting is generally good for knee health when it's done with a range and load that suits you. If you feel discomfort, try squatting to a higher box, adjusting your stance width or slightly turning your toes out. Sharp pain, swelling or pain that lingers is a sign to stop and check with your doctor or physical therapist.

Why it's worth practicing

Strong legs make nearly everything easier, from stairs and hills to getting up off the floor. They also support balance and help protect you from falls. Few exercises pay off as directly in daily life as the squat.
