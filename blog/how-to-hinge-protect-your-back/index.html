The Hip Hinge: The Move That Protects Your Back for Life

Picking things up off the floor is one of the most common ways people tweak their backs. Learning to hinge from the hips changes that.

Think about how often you bend down in a day: grabbing a laundry basket, lifting a suitcase into the car, picking up a grandchild or a bag of dog food. Each of those is a hip hinge. Done well, it uses your strongest muscles. Done poorly, it puts your lower back in charge of work it was never meant to do alone.

The hip hinge is the foundation of the deadlift, but you don't need a barbell to benefit. Learning the pattern makes everyday life safer and builds strength where many adults need it most.

What a hinge actually is

A hinge means bending at your hips while keeping your back long and flat, with only a soft bend in your knees. Your hips move back, your chest tips forward, and the work comes from your glutes and hamstrings. It's different from a squat, where your knees bend more and your hips drop down.

How to practice it

  • Stand with feet about hip-width apart and hands on your hips
  • Soften your knees slightly and keep them there
  • Push your hips straight back, as if closing a car door with your backside
  • Let your chest tip forward while your back stays long and flat
  • Stop when you feel a stretch in the back of your thighs
  • Squeeze your glutes and drive your hips forward to stand tall

Start with 2 sets of 8 slow reps. If your back starts to round or you feel it in your lower back instead of your hamstrings, shorten the range and try again.

A helpful drill: the wall touch

Stand with your back to a wall, about a foot away. Hinge back until your backside lightly touches the wall, then stand up. As you get comfortable, step a little farther from the wall. This teaches your body to move from the hips instead of bending through the spine.

Common mistakes

  • Rounding the back to reach lower
  • Bending the knees so much that it turns into a squat
  • Looking straight up, which strains the neck; keep your gaze a few feet ahead on the floor
  • Rushing; slow, controlled reps teach the pattern best

Taking it into daily life

Once the movement feels natural, use it every time you pick something up. Keep the object close to your body, hinge at the hips, brace your midsection and stand up by squeezing your glutes. Your back will thank you.

Progressing safely

From there, a coach can add a kettlebell or dumbbell, then progress to variations like Romanian deadlifts as your strength grows. Getting feedback on your form early makes a big difference, because a hinge that looks right can still feel wrong, and small adjustments matter.
