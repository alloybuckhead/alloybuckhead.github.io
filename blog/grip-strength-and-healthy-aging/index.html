Grip Strength: A Small Muscle Group With a Big Role in Healthy Aging

How well you can hold on says a lot about your overall strength. Here's why grip matters and how to train it.

Opening a tight jar, carrying grocery bags from the car, holding a handrail on the stairs, pulling yourself up from a low chair: so much of daily life depends on your grip. Yet it's something most people never think about training.

Grip strength is often used in health research as a simple marker of overall muscular strength, and stronger grip has been associated with healthier aging. You don't need to read the studies to see why: if you can hold on firmly, you can do more on your own.

Why grip weakens

Like other muscles, the muscles of the hands and forearms lose strength when they aren't challenged. Modern life asks very little of them. We carry less, type more and rarely hang or pull. Arthritis and old injuries can also make people avoid using their hands, which creates a cycle of further weakness.

The good news: grip responds quickly

Grip is very trainable, and many of the best exercises for it are ones you'd do anyway in a well-rounded strength program. Any time you hold a weight in your hands, your grip is working.

Simple ways to build a stronger grip

  • Farmer's carries: hold a weight in each hand and walk tall for 20 to 40 seconds
  • Suitcase carries: the same, but with a weight in just one hand, which also challenges your core
  • Dead hangs: hang from a bar with feet supported if needed, building up to 10 to 30 seconds
  • Rows and deadlift variations, gripping the handle firmly
  • Squeezing a tennis ball or stress ball, 10 slow squeezes per hand

Start light and focus on standing tall with your shoulders down and back. Increase the weight or time gradually, just as you would with any other exercise.

Everyday grip practice

  • Carry your groceries in from the car instead of using a cart to the door
  • Use the stairs and hold the rail firmly
  • Open your own jars and bottles
  • Do some gardening or yard work

Two or three short grip exercises added to the end of your strength sessions, a couple of times a week, is plenty for most people.

If your hands hurt

If you have arthritis, carpal tunnel symptoms or pain in your hands or wrists, check with your doctor or a hand therapist before adding grip work. Thicker handles, lighter weights and shorter holds are often easier on sore joints. A coach can help find variations that work for you.

Strength you can feel every day

Grip is a great example of how strength training pays off outside the gym. Stronger hands make daily tasks easier and help you stay independent for longer.
